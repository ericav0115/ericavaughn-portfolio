Case study 03 · AI / ML · Vector search

AI Comparable Title Selection

A hybrid system that layers vector search on top of hard business-rule filters to return ranked, explainable comparable titles on demand. It works alongside a third-party supply chain planning platform.

01 · The challenge

The comps drove real decisions. The logic was a black box.

In publishing, a comp is a comparable title whose past performance is used to plan a new one. Comps drive both marketing and supply chain decisions.

In the existing planning platform, comp selection ran on overnight batches, the selection logic was opaque, business users needed IT to make overrides, and licensing cost was significant.

02 · What I did

What I owned and built.

  • Designed a hybrid architecture. SQL hard filters enforce the non-negotiable business rules first (channel routing, key attribute, budget category, merchandising subject), then vector search matches on theme, tone, audience, and other qualitative attributes.
  • Built a comp-lookup tool that takes batches of UPC/ISBN codes and returns ranked comparable titles, each with an explanation of the match.
  • Encoded channel integrity (a retailer-exclusive title only compares to titles from the same channel) and a rule that a title is never its own comp, which matters for re-releases that share a UPC.
  • Presented the system to the marketing team and used their “not another tool” feedback to steer delivery toward automated outputs inside their existing workflow.
  • Positioned the work as complementary to the existing platform rather than a replacement, and submitted a 40-minute breakout proposal to a major data & AI conference.

04 · Key decisions

The judgment behind the build.

Why filters before vectors?

Business rules that can’t be broken shouldn’t be left to probability. Hard filters guarantee eligibility, and vector similarity only ranks within the safe set.

Why explain every match?

Planners won’t trust a comp they can’t reason about. A reason on every result builds confidence and makes overrides informed instead of arbitrary.

Why complement instead of replace?

Replacing a core planning platform is a multi-year conversation. Complementing it delivers value now and builds a path to lower licensing cost later.

Why automated outputs instead of a new UI?

Users told me plainly they didn’t want another tool. Meeting them inside their existing workflow is what actually drives adoption.
