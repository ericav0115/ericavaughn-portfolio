Retail Event Overlap Allocation

A lift-weighted allocation method that stopped overlapping retail events from inflating POS units, so performance comparisons reflect what actually sold.

01 · The challenge

Nine units sold. Sixty-three reported.

The business measures how products perform under different retail events (planograms, bestseller placements, and promotions) by joining point-of-sale data to event records.

When a product-store-week fell under several events at once, POS units were counted once per event row. In one real example, 9 units actually sold showed up as 63 across 7 overlapping rows. About 30% of all POS units fall in overlapping weeks, which was enough to distort any performance comparison.

02 · What I did

What I owned and built.

  • Started with SQL that spread POS evenly across overlapping events using CTEs and window functions, and built step-by-step queries to surface real overlap cases.
  • Designed a lift-weighted proportional allocation in PySpark: a baseline from non-overlapping weeks, then chain-level median lift weights from clean single-event weeks (planogram ≈ 0.89, bestseller ≈ 0.83, promotion ≈ 1.10) used to split the true units across overlapping rows.
  • Fixed a duplication bug by correcting the join keys to chain, store, UPC, and week, and stabilized results by materializing the view as a table.
  • Built an executive presentation package that explains the method in plain language, grounded in a single real record.
  • Kept methodology claims accurate, including correcting an early statement about how two event types compared.

04 · Key decisions

The judgment behind the build.

Why lift-weighted instead of an even split?

Events don’t contribute equally. Weighting by observed lift attributes units in proportion to how each event type actually behaves.

Why median lift?

Outlier weeks would skew a mean. Chain-level medians from clean single-event weeks keep the weights robust.

Why materialize the view?

Recomputing a complex view on every read produced unstable results and slow queries. A table made the numbers reproducible.

Why anchor the story in one record?

“9 became 63” lands with executives in a way a methodology slide never will.
